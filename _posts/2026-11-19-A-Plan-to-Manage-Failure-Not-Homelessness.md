**Housing is the issue ... homelessness is the outcome.**

On November 17, every member of the Chico City Council voted to adopt the City’s Unhoused Strategic Plan. It was a 7–0 vote. Even the people who have spoken most loudly about the dangers of enforcement ultimately signed on to a plan that puts enforcement on a faster and firmer track than housing.

The City calls this a “guiding document.” That is exactly the problem. A guiding document can make broad promises without committing to the housing, money, locations, deadlines, and accountability needed to keep people from living outside.

## What Chico Actually Approved

The plan begins after the *Warren* settlement expires on January 14, 2027. Its first-year priorities include closing the Alternative Camping Site, creating a citywide encampment-response protocol and task force, mapping priority enforcement sites, tracking whether cleared sites stay clear, and expanding enforcement under the revised camping ordinance.

The Council also approved $300,000 in opioid-settlement money connected to implementation:

- **$200,000** for Public Works operations, overtime, and possible contractors.
- **$100,000** for Police staffing and overtime.

The staff report describes that money as immediate funding for outreach and enforcement activities to “quickly address public spaces” and accelerate the City’s enforcement model.

At the same meeting, the Council finalized changes to the camping ordinance. The ordinance expands the areas covered to include waterways and levees, allows misdemeanor or infraction charges, administrative citations, and civil actions, and says an area cleared of camp property is intended to remain clear. It also provides only one warning and one assessment for people subject to the ordinance.

The message is hard to miss: Chico has firm action, money, staff, and deadlines ready for enforcement.

## What It Does Not Commit To

The housing portions are not written with that same certainty.

The plan does **not** commit the City to a number of permanent supportive-housing units, extremely-low-income units, shelter beds, or low-barrier interim spaces. It does not identify sites. It does not attach a housing-production budget. It does not guarantee a safe place to go before someone is forced to leave the place where they are surviving.

Instead, the plan says the City may support housing options “where partners and funding make them feasible,” pursue future grants, recruit landlords, and design a flexible housing-development fund. Its immediate housing-related action is largely to *design*, *evaluate*, *coordinate*, *track*, and *research*.

Those are not bad things. They are simply not housing.

Meanwhile, the Alternative Camping Site is slated for closure in the first two quarters of 2027. The stated deliverable is individualized transition offers, not a guaranteed safe and appropriate placement for every person affected. A roving safe-parking option is listed with timing “to be established” and funding from faith-based partners. Backup hotel, mobile-home voucher, and bed options are to be evaluated.

That is not a reliable safety net before the enforcement system begins moving people.

## The Plan Contradicts Itself

The City says community input shortened the plan from five years to two years and raised the goal of reducing unsheltered homelessness from 25 percent to 50 percent. But the adopted materials are full of conflicting timelines.

The resolution calls it a three-year plan for 2027 through 2029. The cover calls it a “two-year framework” for those same years. The outcomes section still calls itself “five year proposed outcomes” and sets the 50-percent reduction goal against the 2031 Point-in-Time Count. Elsewhere, the document describes the target as a four-year goal.

The staff report also says that public input added a new Action A18 to research support for managed camps, tiny homes, day centers, and other housing options. But the actual A18 listed in the action table is: “Return with Year 2 action plan and budget.” The proposed housing research action is not there as the promised A18.

These may look like editing mistakes. But when a plan is being used to justify closing a site, expanding enforcement, and spending public money, the details are not minor.

A plan cannot be both a two-year plan, a three-year plan, a four-year goal, and a five-year outcome without the public asking which promise is real and who is responsible for meeting it.

## Accountability Cannot Wait Six Months

The Council added a six-month update. That is better than no update, but six months is a long time when enforcement will begin in January, the Alternative Camping Site may close by midyear, and people are being told that public spaces must remain clear.

The City is demanding compliance from people experiencing homelessness now. It should accept the same standard.

Before enforcement expands, the City should publicly commit to:

1. A specific number of permanent supportive and extremely-low-income housing units, with sites, funding sources, and deadlines.
2. Enough low-barrier interim options for people who cannot immediately enter permanent housing, including people with partners, pets, mobility needs, medical needs, vehicles, and other real-world barriers.
3. No closure of the Alternative Camping Site until every person there has an actual safe, appropriate option available, not just a referral or an offer that may not fit.
4. Monthly public reporting on housing placements, returns to homelessness, available beds, people turned away, spending, citations, property removals, and the outcomes of every encampment closure.
5. A standing lived-experience body with real influence over the plan, not a one-time listening meeting after the important decisions have already been made.
6. A clear rule that enforcement is never treated as a substitute for housing.

The City should measure success by how many people obtain and keep housing, not by how many places look clear for seven, 30, or 90 days after a cleanup.

People experiencing homelessness did not create Chico’s shortage of deeply affordable housing. They did not decide that shelter access would have barriers. They did not choose to make a survival crisis into a criminal or administrative enforcement problem.

Chico has once again chosen to manage the visible result of its housing failures while leaving the root problem open-ended. The new Council has the opportunity, and the responsibility, to change that.

Make the promises specific. Fund the housing. Build the alternatives. Report monthly. Then ask the public to judge the results.

Because accountability should apply to the City just as much as it is being demanded from people who have nowhere else to go.
